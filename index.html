Before Forgetting, Learn to Remember: Revisiting Foundational Learning Failures in LVLM Unlearning Benchmarks

JuneHyoung Kwon1, MiHyeon Kim3, Eunju Lee2, JungMin Yun1, Byeonggeuk Lim2, YoungBin Kim1,2
1Department of Artificial Intelligence, Chung-Ang University
2Graduate School of Advanced Imaging Sciences, Multimedia and Film, Chung-Ang University
3KT Corporation
Findings of ACL 2026
Paper 🤗 Dataset

Abstract

While Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) offer powerful capabilities, they pose privacy risks by unintentionally memorizing sensitive personal information. Current unlearning benchmarks attempt to mitigate this using fictitious identities but overlook a critical stage 1 failure: models fail to effectively memorize target information initially, rendering subsequent unlearning evaluations unreliable. Diagnosing under-memorization and the multi-hop curse as root causes, we introduce ReMem, a Reliable Multi-hop and Multi-image Memorization Benchmark. ReMem ensures robust foundational learning through principled data scaling, reasoning-aware QA pairs, and diverse visual contexts. Additionally, we propose a novel Exposure metric to quantify the depth of information erasure from the model's internal probability distribution. Extensive experiments demonstrate that ReMem provides a rigorous and trustworthy framework for diagnosing both learning and unlearning behaviors in LVLMs.

Stage 1 Failure Analysis

(a) Stage 1 memorization on FIUBench, MLLMU-bench and ReMem. (b) Min-k% probability vs. inverse perplexity. (c, d) Causal tracing heatmaps for a FIUBench sample and a Real Set sample.

Unlearning benchmarks assume that the model has memorized the fictitious identities before it is asked to forget them. On existing benchmarks this assumption fails. After fine-tuning, LLaVA stays far below the 100% target on ROUGE, GPT-score and EM (a), and treats fictitious names as low-probability tail events, unlike real public figures it already knows (b). Causal tracing shows no coherent memorization circuit for fictitious identities (c), whereas real identities are retrieved from distinct layers (d). The information was never encoded, so evaluating its erasure is unreliable.

Key Factors for Memorization

(a) Memorization vs. number of QA samples. (b) Perplexity vs. number of QA samples. (c) EM vs. single-hop ratio.

We trace the failure to two causes. Memorization improves with more QA samples per identity until about 160 samples, after which perplexity spikes, indicating overfitting (a, b). Existing benchmarks provide only 1 to 20 samples per identity. They also rely almost entirely on multi-hop questions, which require recognizing the face before retrieving the attribute. Adding single-hop questions as a scaffold helps both question types, with performance peaking at a 70% single-hop ratio (c).

ReMem

Overview of the ReMem construction pipeline.

ReMem generates 20 fictitious profiles with Gemini 2.5 and pairs each identity with 100 template-based QA pairs at a 70:30 single-to-multi-hop ratio. Multi-view images with varied poses, clothing and backgrounds are synthesized from an anchor image with Nano Banana and filtered by ArcFace similarity to keep the identity consistent. The resulting 2,560 samples are split into retain and forget sets, and a held-out test set with unseen QA templates and visual layouts measures out-of-distribution forgetting.

Results

Stage 1. Fine-tuning on ReMem lifts EM from 13.33 to 91.50 (7B) and from 11.25 to 96.37 (13B), with strong held-out test EM, so the identities are genuinely learned rather than overfit.

Stage 2. On this reliable foundation, five unlearning methods reveal a clear utility–forgetting trade-off: GD preserves utility best, while NPO forgets most aggressively but its high Exposure suggests surface-level masking. Multi-hop utility degrades more than single-hop, and the larger 13B model is harder to make forget.
